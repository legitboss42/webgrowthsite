## What should small-business SEO cost each month?

There is no honest monthly figure that fits every small business. The work can change with the condition of the website, the locations and services it needs to cover, the quality of its existing pages, and how much implementation is included. A number without that scope tells you very little.

Web Growth does not publish a fixed monthly SEO retainer price on its public pricing page. Its listed packages are for website projects, so they should not be treated as SEO rates. Ask for a scoped proposal based on your site and goals instead of assuming that a website package or a generic online price is the right comparison.

That does not mean you should accept an unexplained quote. Ask the provider to show the work, the expected cadence, what your team must supply, and how progress will be reported. A clear scope makes proposals easier to compare even when their totals differ.

## Why monthly SEO scopes differ

### The website’s starting condition

A new site, a site with unclear page structure, and an established site with useful content need different first steps. A provider may need to review indexability, page targeting, technical issues, internal links, and existing content before recommending ongoing work. Ask which findings are included in the initial review and which would become separate implementation work.

### The services and locations you want to reach

A business serving one city with a small set of services has a different page and research need from a company serving several locations or customer groups. Clarify which services and locations are in scope. Do not assume that “SEO” automatically includes writing every location page, updating every service page, or managing a business profile.

### Advice versus implementation

Some engagements focus on research, priorities, and recommendations. Others include implementation, such as editing page content, improving internal links, or coordinating technical fixes. A proposal should name the tasks the provider will complete and identify work that depends on your developer, staff, hosting provider, or other suppliers.

### The reporting and decision cycle

Useful reporting should help you decide what to do next. Ask which measures will be reviewed, how often, where the data comes from, and what decisions the report is meant to support. Do not treat a ranking screenshot or a long task list as proof of business outcomes.

Google’s guidance recommends useful, reliable content and descriptive words in prominent page locations, while noting that no particular tactic guarantees inclusion or a specific position in Search. Treat SEO as ongoing improvement and measurement, not a guaranteed ranking product. [Google Search Essentials](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/essentials)

## What to ask for in a monthly SEO proposal

Use these questions to make the scope concrete:

1. **What will you review first?** Ask for the pages, technical areas, and business information needed to understand your starting point.
2. **Which outcomes matter to this business?** Agree on observable indicators such as qualified enquiries or relevant organic landing-page activity where measurement is available. Do not agree to a ranking or revenue guarantee.
3. **What work will you deliver each month?** Request named activities and tangible outputs. “Optimisation” alone is too broad to evaluate.
4. **What is excluded?** Clarify development, new photography, paid promotion, subscriptions, copy approvals, and work beyond the agreed service or location scope.
5. **Who supplies access and approvals?** List the people responsible for analytics access, factual review, technical changes, and sign-off.
6. **How will you show progress and change priorities?** Agree on a reporting rhythm and a process for explaining delays, findings, or changes in direction.
7. **Can the work be reviewed before it goes live?** Content and technical changes should have a clear approval path, especially where they affect customer-facing pages.

A provider should be able to explain the difference between reviewing a problem, recommending a fix, and implementing it. If the proposal promises outcomes but does not describe the work or what depends on you, ask for a clearer version before comparing prices.

## How to compare proposals fairly

Compare the same period and the same responsibilities. Put each proposal beside the questions above and mark whether it includes research, on-page work, technical recommendations, content, implementation, reporting, and stakeholder support. Record exclusions and dependencies next to the price rather than trying to infer them.

Then compare the provider’s proposed first steps with your actual business priorities. If your service pages do not explain what you offer, a plan that only discusses broad keyword targets may not address the immediate gap. If pages and tracking already exist, a technical or measurement review may be more useful first. These are scoping decisions, not universal rules.

## Red flags to question before signing

- **Guaranteed positions or a fixed amount of revenue.** Search visibility and sales depend on many factors outside a provider’s control. Ask what work is committed and what will be measured instead.
- **A large list of activities with no connection to your goals.** Ask why each activity matters for your services, customers, and current site.
- **No explanation of what is included.** Ask for deliverables, cadence, exclusions, and dependencies in writing.
- **Pressure to hand over accounts without a clear access plan.** Keep business-owned accounts under your control and grant only the access needed for agreed work.
- **A proposal that treats content volume as the whole strategy.** Ask how topics match customer questions and how each page supports a useful next step. Google advises creating content for people and warns against producing content primarily to attract search visits without adding value. [Creating Helpful, Reliable, People-First Content](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/fundamentals/creating-helpful-content)
- **A price comparison with no like-for-like scope.** A low headline total may exclude implementation or reporting; a higher total may include work another quote leaves out. Verify before deciding.

## A simple decision checklist

Before you choose a provider, make sure you can answer “yes” to these questions:

- Do I know which services, pages, and locations the work covers?
- Can I see what the provider will do and what they will deliver?
- Do I know what is excluded and what my team must do?
- Is the reporting tied to relevant business questions, with data limits stated?
- Can I review proposed public changes before they are made?
- Have I compared the same work across proposals?

If any answer is no, request clarification. It is reasonable to delay a decision until scope is clear.
