## What should a small-business website maintenance plan include?

A useful plan should match the site and the business’s ability to manage it. At a minimum, the agreement should explain who checks the site, what gets checked, how issues are reported, what response process applies, and which work is outside the recurring scope. “We keep your website running” is not specific enough to compare.

The right schedule depends on the website’s platform, features, update process, and business importance. A simple brochure site does not have the same operational needs as a site with payments, bookings, member accounts, or frequent content changes. Start with what the site does and what would interrupt your customers if it stopped working.

Web Growth lists Website Maintenance and Support as a service, but its public [pricing page](/pricing/) presents website build packages rather than a standard maintenance subscription. Ask for a plan based on your site and needs; this guide does not set a market rate.

## The recurring checks to discuss

### Backups and recovery

Ask whether backups are included, how often they run, where they are kept, and how restoration would be handled if something goes wrong. A backup that has never been checked may not give you confidence that recovery will work. Agree who can authorize restoration and who will tell you if a backup fails.

![Hands hold an external drive beside a laptop and a backup checklist.](/images/blog/website-maintenance-backups.webp)

### Platform, plugin, and theme updates

If your site uses WordPress, updates may apply to core software, themes, and plugins. WordPress documentation recommends keeping software current and making a current backup before updating plugins because problems can occur during an update. This is why a maintenance plan should say how updates are selected, tested, completed, and reviewed rather than simply promising that everything is “always updated.” [WordPress: Manage Plugins](https://wordpress.org/documentation/article/manage-plugins/) and [WordPress: Updating WordPress](https://wordpress.org/documentation/article/updating-wordpress/)

The timing and process should fit the site. Ask if higher-risk changes are tested away from the live site, how compatibility problems are handled, and whether your team must approve a change that affects a customer-facing feature.

### Availability and visible checks

Ask what the provider checks to confirm that key pages and customer paths are available. Name the important paths for your business, such as a contact form, booking flow, or checkout, and agree how failures are reported. A monitoring notification is useful only when someone owns the next action.

### Forms, links, and content changes

Agree whether the plan includes checking enquiry forms, fixing broken links, replacing outdated business details, or making small content edits. Define what counts as a small change, how many requests can be made, and whether new pages or redesign work need a separate quote.

### Performance and technical issues

Clarify whether the provider reviews performance signals or only responds when you report a slow page. If a performance problem needs investigation or development, ask how diagnosis, recommendations, and implementation are priced and approved. Do not assume every technical fix is included in a routine care plan.

### Reporting and support

A short report can record checks completed, issues found, actions taken, and open decisions. Ask how often you will receive it, where requests should go, and what acknowledgement or response terms apply. Do not accept a response-time promise unless it is written into the agreement and the provider confirms it.

## Choose a schedule based on risk, not a generic calendar

Different checks may need different timing. An update check, a backup review, an enquiry-path test, and a content review do not necessarily need the same frequency. Work with the provider to set a schedule that reflects how often the site changes, the consequences of an outage, available backup and recovery arrangements, and your own approval process.

For example, a site that accepts orders may need closer attention to its purchase path than a site that mainly provides opening hours and contact details. This is a planning example, not a universal service standard. The provider should explain why each check is scheduled as proposed.

## Questions to ask before you agree

1. **What exact checks are included?** Ask for a written list and schedule.
2. **What counts as an incident?** Define how you report a problem and who is contacted.
3. **What response terms apply?** Clarify acknowledgement, investigation, and resolution expectations separately.
4. **Are updates tested before they affect customers?** Ask how backups, compatibility, and rollback are handled.
5. **Which fixes are included?** Separate routine work from new features, redesigns, or development.
6. **Who owns access and accounts?** Keep business ownership clear and document the minimum access needed by a provider.
7. **What will the report show?** Ask for checks, findings, actions, unresolved issues, and decisions needed from you.
8. **How can the plan change or end?** Confirm notice, handover, backup delivery, and access removal procedures.
9. **What does it cost, and what is excluded?** Request the recurring fee, any setup work, third-party subscriptions, and the process for approving extra work in writing.

A clear proposal helps you see whether the plan is preventative care, on-call support, small content changes, or a combination. Those are different scopes and should be described separately.

## When maintenance is not enough

Maintenance helps manage routine care. It may not solve a site that has a deeper structural, usability, content, or technical problem. If the same issue returns, key customer tasks are difficult, or you are unsure what should be fixed first, ask for a diagnostic review before expanding the maintenance scope.

Web Growth’s [Website Audit and Consultation service](/services/website-audit/) is a separate option for reviewing a site and identifying priorities. A review is not a promise that every recommended change is included in ongoing maintenance; confirm the deliverables and implementation costs before work begins.

## A simple monthly review checklist

Use this as a conversation starter with your provider. Adapt the checks to your platform and site:

- Were scheduled backups completed, and is the recovery process understood?
- Were planned updates reviewed and completed under the agreed process?
- Do the customer paths that matter still work?
- Were new issues recorded with an owner and next step?
- Are business details and critical pages still accurate?
- Does the report show what was checked, what changed, and what needs approval?
- Is any repeated issue a sign that the site needs a separate audit or development task?

This checklist does not certify that a site is secure or error-free. It creates a regular point to review responsibilities and open work.
