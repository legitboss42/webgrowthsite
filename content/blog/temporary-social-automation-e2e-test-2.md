# A Simple 15-Minute Lead Follow-Up Workflow for Small Businesses

A new enquiry arrives from your website at 11:20 AM.

The customer has already explained what they need. Their name, email address, requested service, and message are sitting in a form submission. The difficult part is not collecting the information. The difficult part is making sure somebody actually acts on it.

For a small team, a useful automation can be surprisingly simple: acknowledge the enquiry, record it, assign it, wait for a defined period, and escalate only if nobody has taken ownership.

For this example, we will use a 15-minute follow-up window. That number is not a universal business rule. It is simply short enough to make the workflow easy to understand and test.

## Step 1: Capture the Enquiry Once

The workflow begins when a customer submits a form, sends a qualified message, or completes another lead action.

The first job is to save the information somewhere reliable. That may be a CRM, a shared inbox, a database, or another system your team already uses.

Avoid copying the same lead into five disconnected tools unless each copy has a clear purpose. More software does not automatically create a better process. Sometimes it merely gives the same forgotten enquiry five different places to hide.

The [website tracking setup guide](/blog/website-tracking-setup-for-small-businesses/) is useful when you also want to record where the enquiry came from and which marketing activity produced it.

## Step 2: Send a Clear Acknowledgement

The customer should know that the enquiry arrived successfully.

A short automated acknowledgement can confirm receipt and set a realistic expectation for the human response. It should not pretend that a staff member personally wrote the message if nobody has reviewed the enquiry yet.

For example, the acknowledgement can say that the request has been received and that the team will respond during the stated business hours.

That simple message removes uncertainty while the internal workflow continues.

## Step 3: Assign a Real Owner

Every lead needs a next owner.

If the enquiry is about website design, it might go to a sales queue. A maintenance request might go to support. An automation enquiry might need somebody who understands the customer's existing tools before replying.

The routing rule should stay understandable. A team member should be able to answer the question, "Why was this lead assigned here?" without needing to reverse-engineer an artificial-intelligence prophecy.

The [email automation architecture guide](/blog/email-automation-architecture/) covers the same principle in more detail: triggers are useful only when exits, ownership, and human handoffs are also defined.

## Step 4: Start the Follow-Up Timer

Once the lead has an owner, start the example 15-minute timer.

The timer is not there to punish the person responsible. It is there to tell the system when to check whether the next action happened.

A useful check might ask:

- Has somebody opened or claimed the enquiry?
- Has a reply been sent?
- Has the lead been moved to a clear next stage?
- Has the owner deliberately postponed it with a reason?

If the answer shows that the lead is already being handled, the automation stops. There is no benefit in sending reminders simply because the software enjoys hearing itself beep.

## Step 5: Escalate Only When Nothing Happened

If the follow-up window expires and the enquiry still has no owner action, then escalate.

The escalation could be a notification in the team's main workspace, a task assigned to a backup person, or a message to the person supervising the queue.

The important word is **only**.

A badly designed workflow sends reminders even when the lead has already been handled. That creates alert fatigue, and eventually everybody learns to ignore the automation. At that point the system has successfully automated being annoying.

## Step 6: Record the Outcome

The final step is to record what happened.

Useful data includes the time the enquiry arrived, when it was assigned, when a human first acted, whether escalation was required, and the eventual outcome.

This creates something much more valuable than a pile of automation logs. It creates evidence about where the process actually slows down.

If a business regularly discovers that enquiries are waiting for hours, the answer may be better routing, clearer ownership, different staffing, or a simpler intake process. Automation should expose the bottleneck rather than disguise it.

## Keep the First Version Small

The first workflow does not need sentiment analysis, ten AI agents, seventeen branching conditions, and a dashboard that looks like mission control.

Start with the boring sequence:

1. Enquiry arrives.
2. Receipt is confirmed.
3. Lead is stored.
4. Owner is assigned.
5. Timer starts.
6. The workflow stops if somebody acts.
7. The workflow escalates if nobody acts.
8. The result is recorded.

Once that works reliably, more sophisticated logic can be added where there is a real reason for it.

## What Should Stay Human?

The automation should not automatically make promises it cannot verify.

Unusual pricing questions, complaints, complex project requirements, exceptions to policy, and sensitive conversations still benefit from human judgement.

A useful workflow gets the right information to the right person quickly. It does not need to impersonate that person.

## FAQ

### Does every enquiry need a reply within 15 minutes?

No. The 15-minute interval is only the example used here. A business should choose a response target that matches its working hours, staffing, service type, and customer expectations.

### Should the automation send the complete sales response itself?

Usually not in the first version. A safer starting point is to acknowledge receipt, capture context, assign ownership, and remind the right person when a human response is still required.

## Next Best Action

Take one enquiry process you already use and write down what happens from the moment the message arrives until somebody owns the next action. If there is a repeated gap between those two points, that is a useful place to test a small automation workflow.
